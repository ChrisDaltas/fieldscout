## Lesson 1 — Why touchdowns lie

**Slug:** `why-touchdowns-lie` · **3 min** · Metrics: `target-share`, `receiving-touchdowns`, `expected-touchdowns`

Touchdowns win you weeks. They just don't tell you anything about next year.

A receiver's touchdown rate is the **least repeatable number in football**. Not "somewhat unreliable" — close to a coin flip. The same guy, in the same offense, running the same routes, can catch 3 one season and 12 the next.

> Cooper Kupp did exactly that, and half the league let him go for it.

### What happened

In 2020 Kupp saw **124 targets** — genuine WR1 volume — and scored 3 touchdowns. The takes wrote themselves: possession guy, not a scorer, aging out. He went in the fourth round of 2021 drafts.

| | 2020 | 2021 |
|---|---|---|
| **Touchdowns** | **3** | **16** |
| Targets | 124 | 191 |
| Catches | 92 | 145 |
| Yards | 974 | 1,947 |

He won the triple crown and the fantasy title for whoever drafted him. **Nothing about his role had changed.** The touchdowns just stopped being unlucky.

### Why it keeps happening

Scoring a touchdown takes a lot of things going right that a receiver doesn't control — field position, play call, whether the running back gets the goal-line carry instead. Over a full season that's still only a dozen or so chances. Small numbers bounce around.

Targets are different. A coach decides who gets the ball hundreds of times a season, and coaches don't change their minds much. **That's why the volume repeats and the scoring doesn't.**

### What to do about it

When someone's touchdown count and their target share disagree, **believe the target share** [link: target-share]. A receiver with a big role and bad luck is the cheapest thing you can buy in fantasy football. A receiver with a small role and great luck is the most expensive thing you can sell.

> **Do this before your next draft**
> Sort last season by touchdowns, then sort by target share. Anyone high on the second list and low on the first is your buy list.

*Sources: box scores via Pro Football Reference. Touchdown-rate stability via Sharp Football Analysis (year-over-year R² 0.008, WR touchdowns per target).*

## Lesson 2 — The stats that repeat

**Slug:** `the-stats-that-repeat` · **4 min** · Metrics: `target-share`, `touches-per-game`, `yards-per-carry`, `points-per-game`

Some numbers tell you what a player will do next year. Most just tell you what already happened.

Opportunity repeats. Efficiency mostly doesn't. Touchdowns are close to a coin flip.

> Draft the guy who gets the ball. Not the guy who did the most with it.

### Volume is the signal

A receiver who saw eight targets a game last year will see something close to that again. Same with a back who touched it 18 times. Roles are decided by coaches, and coaches don't change their minds every August.

That's why targets, touches, carries and snaps are the stats worth chasing. They're the closest thing to a promise the NFL offers.

Points per game repeats too, but only because volume drives it. The role is doing the work underneath.

### Efficiency is mostly noise

Yards per carry barely holds from one season to the next. Neither does yards per target or catch rate. A back at 5.4 a carry is no more likely to repeat it than a back at 4.1.

Touchdown rate is the worst of all. For tight ends it's basically a fresh roll of the dice every year.

So when somebody tells you a guy is due for touchdown regression, they're right. They just usually say it about the wrong player.

### The table

How much each stat repeats from one season to the next. Higher means more repeatable.

| Repeats — draft this | | Doesn't repeat — ignore this | |
|---|---|---|---|
| TE receiving yards per game | 0.609 | TE touchdown rate | 0.0002 |
| RB touches per game | 0.567 | RB yards per touch | 0.008 |
| WR points per game | 0.567 | WR touchdowns per target | 0.008 |
| RB rush attempts per game | 0.562 | WR yards per target | 0.028 |
| QB rushing yards | 0.553 | RB yards per carry | 0.031 |
| WR targets per game | 0.539 | TE yards per target | 0.047 |
| QB completion percentage | 0.498 | QB yards per attempt | 0.092 |
| WR team target share | 0.401 | WR games played | 0.022 |

*Year-over-year R², via Sharp Football Analysis. Higher = repeats more.*

Look at the two touchdown lines. Those are as close to zero as anything in football.

### Why this wins you leagues

Your leaguemates draft last year's box score. They see 14 touchdowns and pay for 14 touchdowns.

You're buying the 150 targets underneath it. Those come back. The scores are a lottery ticket that gets re-drawn every September.

The best version of this edge is the guy who had huge volume and bad luck finishing. He's cheap, and nothing about his job changed.

### What to do about it

Before you take any skill player, ask one question: what is his role, in touches or targets per game?

If you can't answer that, you don't know what you're buying. A great season built on scores you can't explain is a season you shouldn't pay for again.

Flip it for value. Find last year's disappointments who still ate — heavy target counts, weak touchdown totals — and take them a round early. Let someone else have the low-volume touchdown merchants.

For quarterbacks the same rule applies with a twist. Rushing attempts hold better than almost anything a passer does with his arm [link: qb-rushing-yards]. A quarterback who runs is a quarterback with a floor.

> **Do this before your next draft**
> Open your top 24 receivers and top 24 backs. Next to each name, write targets per game or touches per game from last season. Re-rank the list using only that column, then compare it to where they're actually going. Every player you moved up is a guy you should be willing to reach for.

## Lesson 3 — Spotting a breakout early

**Slug:** `spotting-a-breakout-early` · **4 min** · Metrics: `target-share`, `targets-per-game`, `targets-per-route-run`

A player's job changes weeks before his box score catches up. If you wait for the points, you're buying at full price.

> Targets tell you what the coaches decided. Points tell you what already happened.

### Role changes, then production follows

Every week your team has a fixed pile of targets to hand out. Target share is just your guy's slice of that pile [link: target-share].

When his slice grows, that's a decision the coaching staff made. It's the closest thing fantasy has to inside information.

Here's why it matters. Of everything a receiver does, targets per game and target share are the parts that repeat most. Touchdowns per target barely repeat at all.

So a guy scoring on flukey long touchdowns is on borrowed time. A guy quietly climbing to 25% of his team's targets is real.

### The cleanest case: Amon-Ra St. Brown, 2021

St. Brown was a rookie nobody wanted for eleven weeks. Then the Lions handed him the offense, and his final six games looked like this.

| | First 11 games | Final 6 games |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Rotational rookie | Every-down target hog |
| Targets | Low, inconsistent | 10+ every single game |
| Catches | Sporadic | 8+ every single game |
| Yards | Forgettable | 74+ every single game |
| Touchdowns | — | 6 across the stretch |

He has been a top fantasy receiver ever since. But look at his 2021 season line and you see an ordinary rookie, because those first eleven weeks drag the number down.

Anyone reading the trend added him in week 13. Anyone reading the season average passed on him the next summer.

### The trap: season averages describe a player who no longer exists

An average blends the old role and the new one into a number matching neither. It's a rear-view mirror pointed at a guy who already changed jobs.

Say a receiver sits at 14% target share through week 8, then jumps to 28% over the next four. His season average reads about 19%. That's a player who doesn't exist on the field. *(Illustrative example, not a real player.)*

**One big game is not a role change.** A 140-yard blowup on 5 targets is a lucky Sunday. Six targets becoming eight becoming eleven is a promotion.

The tell is the floor, not the ceiling. Ask whether his worst recent week is better than his best week a month ago.

### What DeVonta Smith teaches you

Smith's 2021 rookie season was fine and boring. About 11 PPR points a game, 104 targets, 916 yards.

But he earned targets at a high rate whenever he ran a route. The people who noticed that were a full year ahead of the room.

In 2022 he went for 136 targets, 95 catches, 1,196 yards and 15.3 points per game. The rate was there first. The totals took a season to show up.

### What to do about it

Stop scanning points per game. Scan the last three weeks of targets, in order, and look for a staircase.

Three straight weeks of climbing targets is your buy signal. Add him off waivers before he posts the big line, or offer a name-brand disappointment for him in a trade.

You're not betting on a hot streak. You're betting the coaches meant it.

> **Do this every Tuesday**
> Pull up your league's waiver wire, sort by targets over the last three weeks only, and add the top guy who's under 40% rostered. Ignore his points entirely.

*Sources: box scores via Pro Football Reference. Stability figures via Sharp Football Analysis and SumerSports.*

## Lesson 4 — Reading a rookie

**Slug:** `reading-a-rookie` · **3 min** · Metrics: — *(college metrics are a separate spec; this lesson links out to the position guides)*

Two things tell you most of what you need to know about a rookie: where he got drafted and what he did in college. Almost everything else you hear between January and September is noise.

> The NFL team spent a real pick on him. That's the strongest opinion in the room.

### Draft capital is the whole ballgame

Where a team picks a player tells you how badly they want him on the field. Roughly 73% of 1,000-yard receiver seasons since 2006 came from players drafted in rounds 1–3. That's the single best public signal you have.

Late-round guys can hit. They just hit far less often, and they need injuries in front of them to get the reps.

Treat draft capital as your starting price. Everything else moves you a tier, not a round.

### College production, in plain terms

Two college numbers actually carry over. Both are easy to look up.

**Dominator rating** is the share of his college team's receiving yards and touchdowns he accounted for. 35%+ is elite. 20–35% is middling. Under 20% is a red flag.

**Breakout age** is how old he was in his first season above 20% dominator (15% for tight ends and backs). Younger is much better. Receivers taken in round 1 who broke out at 18 hit a top-24 PPR season about 73% of the time.

The flip side is brutal. Of 72 receivers who broke out after 19 and went outside the top four rounds, three ever hit.

### The checklist, in order

| Priority | What to check | What you want |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draft capital | Rounds 1–3. Round 1 best. |
| 2 | Dominator rating | 35%+ elite, under 20% is a fade |
| 3 | Breakout age | 18–19 great, 21+ needs round 1–2 capital |
| 4 | Landing spot | Thin depth chart ahead of him |
| 5 | Relative Athletic Score | Only care if it's under 5 |

Stop after line three most of the time. Those are the ones that repeat.

### What to ignore

Combine athleticism. An academic study found the combine predicts who makes an NFL roster fairly well, but barely predicts how good he'll be once he's there. There is no published figure showing Relative Athletic Score predicts NFL production, and we won't pretend otherwise.

RAS is a 0–10 score of a prospect's measurables against history at his position. Below 5 is a genuine red flag. Between 5 and 8 tells you nothing at all. Above 8 is a mild positive, nothing more.

Same for Speed Score, which is weight times 200, divided by his 40 time to the fourth power. It adjusts the 40 for size. Nice context, bad reason to move a guy up your board.

Preseason hype is worse. Camp reports and one good August drive move rookie ADP every year. They tell you nothing about September through December.

### What to do about it

Build your rookie board off draft capital first, before you watch a single highlight. Then sort within each tier by dominator rating and breakout age.

Only move a player after that if his athletic score is under 5. That's a fade, not a boost.

When someone at your draft says a fourth-rounder is the steal of the class, let them take him. You're buying the round-2 pick with a 40% dominator who broke out at 19.

> **Do this before your rookie draft**
> Open a blank sheet. For every rookie you might draft, write three columns: draft round, dominator rating, breakout age. Don't write a single word about the combine, and rank the board off those three columns only.

*Sources: draft-capital and hit-rate figures via BrainyBallers and Dynasty League Football; dominator tiers via PlayerProfiler; combine study via arXiv 2303.05774; RAS guidance via Hogs Haven.*

## Lesson 5 — What separation doesn't tell you

**Slug:** `what-separation-doesnt-tell-you` · **2 min** · Metrics: `separation`, `drop-rate`, `catch-rate`, `target-share`

The stats you hear most on broadcasts — separation, cushion, contested catch rate, drop rate — barely move fantasy scoring. They're real football skills. They just don't tell you who scores points.

> Being open is a skill. Being thrown to is a job description.

### Why a real skill can be useless for fantasy

Separation measures how well a receiver does his job on a single play. It tells you almost nothing about how many plays he gets.

Fantasy points come from chances. A receiver with a target is in the scoring business. A receiver who's wide open on a play the quarterback never looks at scores zero.

That's the whole disconnect. Play-quality stats grade the play. Fantasy scoring counts the plays.

So a receiver can be genuinely excellent at getting open and still finish as your WR4. And a slow, badly-separated receiver can finish as a WR1 because his offense funnels him eight looks a game.

### What actually repeats

How well each stat holds up from one season to the next. Higher repeats more.

| Stat | Repeatability |
|---|---|
| Targets per game | 0.70 — strongest on the board |
| Fantasy points per game | 0.68 — strong |
| Average depth of target | 0.65 — strong |
| Targets per route run | 0.64 — strong |
| Touchdown rate | 0.19 — weak |
| Value added per target | 0.18 — weak |
| Drop rate | 0.14 — weak |
| Contested catch rate | 0.02 — tells you nothing |

*Pearson r, 4for4, WRs with 30+ targets, 2017–2023. Note this is r, not the R² used in lesson 2 — the two aren't on the same scale.*

The pattern isn't subtle. Everything about opportunity sits at the top. Everything about play quality sits at the bottom.

### This is not anti-analytics

Completion percentage over expected is the counter-example [link: cpoe]. For quarterbacks it's one of the most reliable public numbers there is. One prior season explains about 23% of the next season. Three seasons gets you to about 40%.

So tracking data can work. The question is always whether a stat measures a repeatable skill that also generates volume.

### What these stats are genuinely good for

Separation and cushion are excellent football information. They explain why a coordinator trusts a receiver on third down. They help scouts tell a route technician from an athlete.

They also explain the past well. When a receiver's numbers spike, contested catch rate and drop rate help you understand what happened. That's real value.

It's just not predictive value for your lineup. Explaining last year and forecasting next year are different jobs.

### What to do about it

Rank by chances first. Targets per game and target share before anything a tracking camera measures.

When a broadcast cites separation, treat it as a description of style, not a buy signal. Ask the follow-up: does his offense throw to him.

Never move a player up your board on drop rate or contested catch rate alone. Use separation to break a tie between two players with similar target volume. That's the correct size of the tool.

> **The one-line version**
> Separation, cushion, drops and contested catches measure how well a receiver does his job — but fantasy points come from how often he gets the chance, so draft the targets, not the technique.

*Sources: 4for4, Sharp Football Analysis, nfelo. Separation and cushion are Next Gen Stats — Tier 2 under spec §2.2, so this lesson ships before the data does.*
